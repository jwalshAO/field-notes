Welcome to Field Notes
Your in-pocket intel capture tool.

Chat with Q by voice or text, hit Done, and your note flows into the team dossier. Every note you submit makes the whole team smarter — facts get pulled out and surfaced on every surgeon, hospital, manufacturer, and competitor page in the CRM.

Why "Q"? After Bond's Q-Branch — your technical sidekick in the field. In and out, no theatrics. Reps are the agents; Q hands you the gear and gets out of your way.

Form or Chat — your call

Two ways to capture: a quick fill-in-the-blanks Form (the default), or Chat with Q by voice or text. Tap either in the toggle up top — the app remembers your choice and opens that way next time.

What to capture — go wide

Everything is intel. Don't filter yourself.

  • Conversations — surgeons, materials managers, OR staff, schedulers, manufacturer reps. Who said what.
  • Hospital intel — staffing, who's tired of which vendor rep, OR scheduling, vendor policies, things you overheard.
  • Schedule signals — "Boz is at PMUC on Mondays." Builds out the surgeon calendar.
  • Product interest — anyone mentioning competitors, Geminus, nerve wraps, distal humerus, etc.
  • Feedback — case wins, complaints, training reactions, tray issues.
  • Competitor sightings — a tray on the loaner rack, a rep talking to a surgeon, Dr. Strange using Acumed for clavicle.
  • Random observations — anything you noticed that you'd otherwise forget.

The more breadth, the richer the team dossier gets.

How it works

  1. Tap a chip on the welcome screen, or just type / hit the mic.
  2. Talk through what you observed/overheard. Q asks at most one follow-up.
  3. Hit Done. Review the auto-organized draft. Hit Submit.

Two taps to save: Done → Submit. In a hurry? Say "submit" or "send it" any time — Q skips its clarifying questions and saves what you have. You can always edit on the confirm screen.

The chips

Six topic chips (Interaction, Hospital, Competition, Opportunity, Feedback, Schedule) plus three contact-type chips (Meeting/Demo, Tend, Give to Grow) plus Other. Use multiple freely — a surgeon demo can be Meeting/Demo + Interaction + Opportunity. Tag less, capture more.

Tend & Give to Grow — the relationship moves

These two chips capture the relationship-building work that drives long-term growth. We track them so the team can see who's investing in which relationships and how those investments pay off.

  • Tend — pure relationship maintenance. Chatting with Battista just to catch up. Stopping by Methodist to say hi. Not transactional, not opportunity-driven — keeping the relationship warm.
  • Give to Grow — you gave value to someone with no ask attached: a thoughtful intro, a relevant article, time spent helping them think through a problem, a referral they needed. Not random — planned and strategic, on a cadence (think quarterly per relationship). The "grow" comes later, naturally, because that's how reciprocity works. What's behind Give to Grow →

Both feed the "what made this relationship strong" record.

No friction promise

  • Q will never block your note.
  • Names not in the CRM get captured as text — no arguing.
  • Q may pause once to confirm a name match ("Jeanie at Methodist — sound right?"). Say yes/no, or just say "submit" to skip and save with what you have.
  • The confirm screen lets you edit anything.
  • Voice works one-handed in the parking lot.
Give to Grow — the philosophy
Mo Bunnell's BD approach, in long form.

Mo Bunnell's "Give to Grow" is the core philosophy behind his business development (BD) work, and it became the title of his 2024 book. The central idea is simple but cuts against how most professionals think about BD: the fastest way to grow your book of business is to focus relentlessly on giving value to others, not on asking for the sale.

A few key threads in how he frames it:

The mindset shift

Most people in professional services (lawyers, consultants, advisors, sales reps) feel awkward about BD because they think of it as taking — asking for time, asking for referrals, asking for the deal. Bunnell reframes the whole activity as giving. When you lead with generosity, the relationship deepens naturally and the business follows. The discomfort largely disappears because you're not doing the thing that felt icky.

What "giving" actually means

It's not just free advice or favors. Bunnell breaks it into categories — sharing insights, making introductions, sending relevant articles or research, offering frameworks, connecting people to opportunities, even just being genuinely curious about someone's problems. The key is that the gift is relevant and tailored, not generic. A thoughtful intro to the right person beats a generic "checking in" email a hundred times.

It's systematic, not random

This is what separates Bunnell from typical "be a nice person" relationship advice. Give to Grows are planned, not spontaneous. Bunnell pairs the mindset with disciplined process: a defined list of the relationships you're investing in, a regular cadence — typically quarterly per relationship — and tracked touches so you can see where each one sits on the path from contact to client to advocate. Generosity at scale requires a system; otherwise it doesn't happen consistently enough to compound.

The compounding effect

Givers build reputations. Over years, the people you've helped remember it, refer you, hire you, and bring you into rooms you couldn't have entered on your own. The growth is non-linear — slow at first, then accelerating — which is why most people quit before it pays off.

Reciprocity isn't the point — but it happens

Bunnell is careful to distinguish this from transactional networking ("I'll help you so you'll help me"). The gift has to be freely given, no strings. But because humans are wired for reciprocity, the returns come anyway, often from unexpected directions.

For Agility specifically

This maps cleanly onto how we build with surgeons and hospital staff. The reps who win long-term aren't the ones pushing for sales; they're the ones surgeons call when they have a weird case, want to think through a technique, or need a connection at another institution. That's Give to Grow in the OR.

His earlier book The Snowball System (2018) covers a lot of the same ground with more emphasis on the process side; Give to Grow is the more mindset-forward, accessible version. Both come out of his firm Bunnell Idea Group (BIG), which trains professional services firms on this methodology.
